Anthropology, as the holistic and comparative study of humankind across time, space, and scale, uniquely insists on integrating biological, cultural, linguistic, and archaeological lines of evidence in order to interrogate what it means to be human under radically diverse historical and ecological conditions, refusing the disciplinary silos that fragment most social sciences and instead foregrounding ethnography—the sustained, reflexive immersion in the lived worlds of others—as both method and epistemic virtue. From its late-nineteenth-century origins in the evolutionary schematizing of Tylor, Morgan, and Frazer, through the historic particularism of Boas and the functionalist syntheses of Malinowski and Radcliffe-Brown, to the structuralism of Lévi-Strauss, the symbolic and interpretive turns inaugurated by Geertz and Turner, the materialist interventions of Harris and the Marxist-inflected political economy of Wolf and Roseberry, and onward into contemporary critical, feminist, postcolonial, decolonial, ontological, and more-than-human anthropologies (Ortner, Abu-Lughod, Tsing, Kohn, Viveiros de Castro, et al.), the discipline has repeatedly demonstrated its capacity for self-critique, dismantling earlier universalist pretensions that masked Eurocentric bias while simultaneously expanding its theoretical repertoire to encompass practice theory, assemblage thinking, multispecies ethnography, and the anthropology of infrastructure, debt, and precarity in an era of neoliberal hegemony and accelerating climate collapse. This reflexive restlessness, combined with an abiding commitment to fine-grained empirical description and the relentless contextualization of power, has allowed anthropology not only to document the extraordinary plasticity of Homo sapiens—our ability to generate incommensurable cosmologies, kinship systems, exchange regimes, and technomaterial assemblages—but also to reveal how ostensibly natural categories (race, gender, economy, nature itself) are historically contingent products of specific regimes of knowledge-power, thereby offering one of the most sustained intellectual challenges to the ontological security of Western modernity and its claims to singular truth, progress, or human exceptionality.
