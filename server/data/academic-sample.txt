There are two broad types of relationships: formal and functional. Formal relationships hold between descriptions. A description is any statement that can be true or false. Example of a formal relationship: The description that a shape is a square cannot be true unless the description that it has four equal sides is true. Therefore, a shape's being a square depends on its having four equal sides. Functional relationships hold between events or conditions. An event is anything that happens in time. Example of a functional relationship: A plant cannot grow without water. Therefore, a plant's growth depends on its receiving water. The first type is structural, meaning it holds between statements about features. The second is operational, meaning it holds between things in the world as they act or change. Descriptions as objects of consideration are always propositional in nature. The objects of evaluation are descriptions that can be assessed for truth or falsity. Something is not evaluated unless it is described, and it is not described unless it can be stated. One can notice non-descriptions such as sounds, objects, and movements in the environment. But in the relevant sense one evaluates descriptions of them rather than the raw phenomena. Relationships not known through direct observation require inference from established premises. Some relationships are known not through direct observation but through reasoning from prior knowledge. Such relationships are structural as opposed to observational in their epistemic character. Examples of structural relationships include logical entailments and necessary conditions for states of affairs. If A then A or B is a basic logical truth that holds necessarily. All tools require some form of use in order to fulfill their intended function. Nothing can be both moving and perfectly still at the same moment in time. There are no rules without conditions to which those rules apply in specific contexts. A tool's being functional depends on its being usable by agents with relevant skills. An object's being both moving and still depends on contradictory conditions which cannot occur together. The existence of rules depends on the existence of conditions to which they meaningfully apply. Structural truth and structural understanding are always understanding of relationships between concepts. Structural understanding is always understanding of relationships rather than isolated facts. Observational understanding can be either direct through perception or indirect through testimony.

It doesn't follow that it avoids more gaps than the one it replaces in explanatory terms. It doesn't follow that it is comparable from a trade-off standpoint to the original hypothesis. In fact the opposite often holds when we examine the details carefully and systematically. Consider the alternative mentioned earlier in our discussion of the primary evidence. The cost of that account meaning what new puzzles it creates is vastly greater than its value. The value of an account is measured by what old puzzles it removes from consideration. It would be difficult to devise an account inconsistent with the conventional one that still matches evidence. You can test this for yourself by attempting to construct such an alternative systematically. If there is reason to think even one such account exists it is not because one has been produced. That reason if it exists must be purely theoretical rather than grounded in empirical observation. And for reasons soon to be made clear no such purely theoretical reason can justify accepting it. But there is a further difficulty for this or any non-standard replacement of the conventional view. It is not at all clear that once the relevant details are considered the replacement is possible. Taken on its own a substitute account may describe a situation that seems coherent and consistent. It may not be contradictory in the strict sense of entailing both a proposition and its negation. But that alone is not enough for it to serve as a viable model of the relevant information. Think of the range of underlying A that would have to be set aside to accept this view. Setting them aside if possible at all would create ripple effects throughout the theoretical framework. Consider the various B that would be altered and the C disrupted as a consequence. Those B do not only sustain the single E in question but many other phenomena as well. Removing them would have many other consequences unrelated to the specific aim of the new model.
When would it become rational to believe that next time you're more likely than not to get this outcome? This belief becomes rational when and only when you have reason to believe that outcome is favored. And that belief in its turn is rational if you know that circumstances like the following obtain. The structures involved in the setup must favor certain outcomes over others systematically. The objects involved must have properties that make certain outcomes more probable than alternatives. On any given trial the probability distribution reflects the underlying causal structure at work. The structural features of the experimental setup determine which outcomes are possible and likely. Probability assessments must be grounded in knowledge of the relevant causal mechanisms operating. Without such grounding probability assignments become arbitrary and lack epistemic justification for agents. The framework developed here provides a principled basis for making such assessments carefully. These considerations apply equally to simple cases and to more complex situations involving multiple variables.
